\title{When Aggregate Welfare Misses Joint-Action Structure in Multi-Agent Pricing}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Aggregate welfare can remain unchanged while a multi-agent system changes how it occupies and shares joint-action states. We quantify this observability boundary in a repeated two-seller pricing environment with public peer-history displays. An exhaustive audit of the finite action grid showed that 100 ordered price states collapsed into 10 classes with identical minimum-price welfare observables, although seller-level allocations differed. In a controlled-initial comparison, the natural-display arm produced equal-price assessment states in 88.8\% and 85.3\% of repeated assessment rounds in the two asymmetric cells, whereas the hide-rival arm produced none. At the independent block level, the corresponding paired equal-price differences were 0.885 and 0.853, while paired minimum-price differences were only 0.011 and 0.014. A fixed-history diagnostic further showed that the response to history order depended on the rival background, with a corrected difference-in-differences of 0.546 price units. Within this settlement rule and observed support, the results identify information-dependent joint-action structure that aggregate welfare alone cannot resolve. A supplementary best-response probe established current-profit competence, whereas a matched forced-deviation probe found no next-round response at the tested operating point. They do not identify collusion or strategic harmful coordination, which would require a benchmark-relative surplus-bearing operating point, a declared deviation and response rule, and a causal counterfactual.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{multi-agent systems, generative agents, coordination, agent evaluation, algorithmic pricing, observability}

\section{Introduction}

Public action histories are an information channel even when a multi-agent system has no explicit message interface. In a market, a seller can condition its current price on a peer's previous price, and that response can change the joint trajectory without any natural-language communication. Such behavior is often summarized by aggregate outcomes: mean price, total welfare, or industry profit. Those summaries are useful, but they do not necessarily identify the joint action that produced them.

The measurement problem is particularly sharp in homogeneous Bertrand environments. The lowest posted price determines demand, consumer surplus, and total industry profit. The same minimum price can therefore be generated by a tie, in which both sellers share demand, or by an asymmetric state, in which one seller captures the market. An intervention can move trajectories between these states while leaving welfare unchanged. A null welfare contrast is then compatible with a large structural change.

We ask when welfare-only monitoring misses changes in joint actions in generative multi-agent systems. We test whether peer-history display changes closed-loop outcomes or joint price-state structure. We also test whether it changes seller-profit allocation and define the evidence needed for a strategic interpretation.

We show that display policy changes joint-state persistence and seller-profit allocation inside minimum-price equivalence classes. The effect appears at the first model decision and persists in the assessment window, while the minimum price changes little. A fixed-history diagnostic provides a complementary semantic test: the action response to history order depends on the rival background. Together, the studies show why outcome aggregates, action structure and strategic response must be evaluated separately.

The paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
    \item We enumerate the action-grid equivalence classes induced by the settlement rule, showing the many-to-one mapping from joint prices to aggregate welfare observables and checking one class against a recorded market round.
    \item We report a controlled-initial closed-loop comparison in which the hide-rival arm changes early and persistent joint-state structure while leaving minimum-price welfare nearly unchanged in asymmetric cells.
    \item We isolate background-dependent history conditioning with a fixed-history diagnostic that does not rely on trajectory-level mediation claims.
    \item We define the boundary of a strategic interpretation: equal-price persistence and high prices are not evidence of harmful coordination without a surplus-bearing operating point, a declared deviation, a response rule, and a causal counterfactual.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarizes the study logic: a display intervention is propagated through model decisions into a joint trajectory, then evaluated at progressively richer evidence surfaces before any strategic interpretation is made.

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Multi-agent learning and emergent communication}

Classical multi-agent research separates individual policies from the organization that emerges through interaction \cite{stone2000multiagent,busoniu2008comprehensive,leibo2017multiagent}. A major line of work makes the interaction channel explicit: agents learn differentiable messages or symbolic protocols through centralized training or end-to-end communication \cite{foerster2016communicate,sukhbaatar2016commnet,havrylov2017emergence}. These studies establish that communication can be learned as part of a joint policy, but their primary observables are task return, message structure, or protocol success. Our setting removes that explicit channel and treats the displayed peer history itself as the intervention. The resulting question is not whether agents can communicate, but whether a monitor can detect joint-state changes when the interface changes without changing the action space.

\subsection{Coordination under mixed incentives}

Coordination can also emerge from the environment and the learning objective rather than from a prescribed language protocol. Centralized-critic methods study mixed cooperative--competitive policies and the effect of observing other agents \cite{lowe2017maddpg}; intrinsic social influence rewards measure how one agent's action changes another agent's future behavior \cite{jaques2019social}; and autocurricula can produce coordinated tool use without a fixed symbolic language \cite{baker2020tooluse}. Together these results show that joint behavior can be structured and history-dependent. They do not, however, make the settlement rule's many-to-one mapping from joint actions to aggregate outcomes an estimand. We add that missing measurement layer by separating aggregate welfare from ordered states, transitions, and seller-level allocation.

\subsection{Algorithmic pricing and collusion claims}

The economic literature provides the closest domain context. Classical work links imperfect observation and public price information to tacit coordination \cite{stigler1964theory,green1984noncooperative,salop1986practices}. Algorithmic-pricing studies show supra-competitive outcomes from dynamic feedback or correlated experimentation \cite{calvano2020ai,hansen2021frontiers,klein2021autonomous}, with field evidence of higher margins after adoption in some competitive markets \cite{assad2024algorithmic}. LLM pricing experiments report high prices, prompt sensitivity, and off-path responses \cite{fish2024algorithmic}. A high or synchronized price nevertheless remains compatible with several mechanisms. Here the minimum price determines welfare while the ordered pair determines who captures demand, so we enumerate the market's equivalence classes before testing structural or allocation changes. This is a measurement distinction, not a stronger collusion claim.

\subsection{Generative and language-model agents}

Generative-agent systems make memory, role assignment, conversation, and tool use part of the interaction interface \cite{park2023generative,li2023camel,wu2023autogen}. ReAct and Reflexion further couple language-model reasoning to action and verbal feedback \cite{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion}, while AgentBench evaluates agents across interactive environments \cite{liu2024agentbench}. These frameworks motivate interface-level interventions, but they generally report task success or benchmark scores rather than whether an aggregate monitor is blind to distinct joint actions. We isolate a minimal display-policy contrast in a controlled economic environment, so the monitor's input surface and the claim it can support are explicit.

\subsection{Positioning of this study}

Prior work supplies coordination mechanisms, economic outcomes, and increasingly capable agent interfaces. The unresolved link is operational: which evidence supports a claim about market harm, which supports a claim about joint-action structure, and which is required before calling that structure strategic harmful coordination? We answer this by combining a full-grid observability enumeration, a controlled-initial closed-loop comparison, and a fixed-history semantic diagnostic. The resulting target map treats welfare, joint structure, allocation, and strategic response as distinct estimands rather than as interchangeable proxies.

\section{Environment and Estimands}

\subsection{Two-seller pricing environment}

Two sellers choose prices from $\{2.0,2.5,\ldots,6.5\}$. Marginal cost is 1, reservation price is 10, and market size is 100. The lowest-priced seller serves all demand, and a tie splits demand equally. Let $m=\min(p_A,p_B)$ and $Q(m)=100(10-m)/9$. Consumer surplus and total industry profit are
\begin{equation}
    CS(m)=\frac{1}{2}(10-m)Q(m), \qquad \Pi(m)=(m-1)Q(m).
\end{equation}
Total welfare is $W(m)=CS(m)+\Pi(m)$. Seller profit allocation depends on the number of sellers attaining $m$. Thus $W$, $CS$, and $\Pi$ are functions of $m$, whereas seller-level rewards and profit HHI also depend on the ordered price pair.

The class count is not a sampling approximation. Enumerating the ten-point action grid gives exactly ten classes: the class with minimum $m$ contains all ordered states whose lower coordinate is $m$, with the ceiling class as a singleton. This arithmetic enumeration states the settlement-induced observability boundary before any model call is interpreted; it is not itself an empirical contribution.

The minimum sufficient augmentation depends on the target. Recording the ordered price pair separates every joint state, while recording seller-level rewards or market shares separates capture from equal division within a non-degenerate minimum-price class. HHI is sufficient for the two endpoints under this two-seller tie-split rule, but it is a derived allocation statistic rather than an independent structural observable. Thus the monitor must expose the primitive needed by the claim it is asked to support.

\subsection{Evidence layers}

We distinguish four evidence targets. Market harm concerns minimum price, welfare, and benchmark gaps. Joint-action structure concerns ordered price-pair distributions, equality, transitions, and persistence. Profit allocation concerns seller-share disparity and equal-price division. Strategic harmful coordination requires a counterfactual deviation gain and a response to that deviation. The first three targets are observable in the present studies. The fourth is not identified.

The block was the independent unit for inferential contrasts. Assessment counts remained descriptive and nested within trajectories. Registered contrasts used block-level intervals with multiplicity correction. Post-hoc simple contrasts and paired sensitivities were labeled separately and did not replace the registered estimands.

\subsection{Target map and identification boundary}

We use a target map to prevent a result at one evidence layer from being promoted to another. Let $f$ denote a monitor's recorded surface and $g$ a scientific target. The target is recoverable from $f$ only if $g$ is constant on every $f$-equivalence class; otherwise an additional observable $h$ is required to split that class. In this environment welfare treatment effects are estimable under the randomized design, but the ordered joint state and its allocation are not recoverable from a welfare-only surface. T1 denotes market harm as measured by minimum price and welfare. T2 denotes joint-action structure, including equality and transitions. T3 denotes allocation and concentration, including equal division versus capture. T4 denotes strategic harmful coordination. The map is coupled to the monitor's input surface: a T1 monitor can be blind to seller-level allocation, while a T2 or T3 monitor still cannot establish a deviation response. In the present environment the grid audit establishes the T1 equivalence classes, the trajectories provide T2/T3 observations, and no behavioral T4 counterfactual is observed. The observed symmetric states do admit supra-competitive surplus relative to the feasible grid-Nash outcome at 2.0, but the present run does not test whether that surplus is strategically sustained.

\section{Study Designs}

\subsection{Closed-loop policy contrast}

The earlier four-arm repeated-pricing study varied whether own and rival price histories were displayed. The registered rival-history factorial effect averages the rival-history manipulation across the own-history condition. We also retain a post-hoc natural-to-hide-rival simple contrast because it asks the direct policy question. The two estimands are reported separately.

For the controlled-initial welfare outcome $Y$, the registered contrasts are defined as
$D_{\mathrm{asym}}=\tfrac{1}{2}[(Y_{LH,\mathrm{hide}}-Y_{LH,\mathrm{natural}})+(Y_{HL,\mathrm{hide}}-Y_{HL,\mathrm{natural}})]$ and
$J=D_{\mathrm{asym}}-\tfrac{1}{2}[(Y_{LL,\mathrm{hide}}-Y_{LL,\mathrm{natural}})+(Y_{HH,\mathrm{hide}}-Y_{HH,\mathrm{natural}})]$.
Both were estimated with Bonferroni-corrected block-level $t$ intervals; the structural sensitivities reported below use a separate natural-minus-hide-rival sign convention.

\subsection{Fixed-history diagnostic}

The fixed-history diagnostic contains 1,458 audited logical units in repeated matched blocks. It varies history content, last values, prefix composition, prefix order, and rival-history visibility while recording the complete joint action. Here the ordering effect is the mean selected price under one prefix order minus the mean selected price under its matched reversal, holding the final own price and rival-background condition fixed. The reported interaction is the difference between that ordering effect under a hidden rival background and under a visible all-6.5 rival background, at own last price 6.0. It is one explicitly labeled comparison from a 42-comparison post-hoc family; the full family is retained in the supplementary analysis table. This is a single-shot semantic-control study. It tests background-dependent action conditioning, not dynamic persistence or punishment.

\subsection{Controlled initial states}

The controlled-initial study programs round-0 prices as one of $(6.0,6.0)$, $(6.0,6.5)$, $(6.5,6.0)$, and $(6.5,6.5)$. The two display arms were named natural-display and hide-rival. Rounds 1--29 used model decisions; rounds 10--29 formed the assessment window. The final record contained 380 completed trajectories and 4 failed trajectories. The run was an amended continuation after an operational stop caused by an error-classification defect; failed trajectories were not repaired.

\subsection{Model, prompt, and reproducibility}

The controlled-initial run used DeepSeek's \texttt{deepseek-flash} model through two separate simultaneous API calls, one per seller. The calls used temperature 0.3, a 256-token limit, forced invocation of the price-selection tool, one format attempt, and no format-repair retry. The system prompt instructed each model to act as an autonomous seller maximizing cumulative profit without a message channel and to select a price through the required tool. Each user message supplied sorted JSON fields for the round index, remaining horizon, costs and reservation value, demand rule, market size, allowed prices, and a projected posted-price history. Crucially, the observation surface did not include realized sales, quantities, seller rewards or profits, consumer surplus, industry profit, HHI, or any other market-outcome statistic; cumulative-profit maximization appeared only as an objective in the system prompt. In the natural-display arm both own and rival posted-price histories were present; in hide-rival the rival price fields were replaced by null while own posted prices remained visible.

There were 30 rounds per trajectory: round 0 was programmed and rounds 1--29 were model decisions. The schedule used seed 20260929, with the environment reset seed equal to the block identifier; no provider-side API seed was recorded. Thus, block pairing matches the deterministic environment reset and schedule, but does not replay provider sampling randomness. The execution snapshot retained 380 completed and 4 unresolved trajectories. The failed cells were LL-natural (blocks 37 and 41), HH-hide-rival (block 37), and LH-hide-rival (block 47); they were not repaired or imputed. All reported denominators therefore come from the analysis ledger, and the source artifact records the execution amendment and missingness explicitly.

\subsection{Offline structural and incentive analyses}

We enumerate the complete 10-by-10 grid and derive the partition induced by $(m,W,CS,\Pi)$. We then check a representative equivalence pair against a real ledger round as an implementation validation. Best-response enumeration gives the unique pure grid-Nash outcome $(2.0,2.0)$, with per-seller profit 44.444; the joint-profit maximum is the symmetric 5.5 state, with per-seller profit 112.500. These benchmarks are descriptive arithmetic, not evidence that the language model computes or follows an equilibrium. A T4 test would additionally require an exogenous deviation and a matched response counterfactual.

\subsection{Capability and deviation-response probes}

Two supplementary probes used the same \texttt{deepseek-flash} endpoint, forced price tool, temperature 0.3, and 256-token limit. In X1, an externally fixed rival price (6.0 or 6.5) and an identical current-profit table made 5.5 the unique current-profit best response; the only randomized prompt content was whether the decision was terminal or had 29 further rounds. In X2, 48 four-arm blocks began with a programmed $(6.0,6.0)$ prelude; at round 10 the designated seller was forced to 5.5 in the intervention arms, while matched no-shock arms preserved the model action. Natural-display and hide-rival conditions used the same observation surfaces as the main study, and no realized outcome was shown. X1 was analysed at the prompt-ID cluster level; X2 was analysed at the complete-block level.

\subsection{Failure handling and analysis order}

The analysis ledger distinguishes planned cells, attempted trajectories, completed trajectories, and unresolved outcomes. We do not impute the four failed trajectories or silently drop their blocks. Descriptive denominators therefore appear next to every repeated-round count. The controlled-initial contrast was computed after the execution amendment and is reported with the amended completion range. The fixed-history diagnostic remains a separate single-shot estimand. This separation is important because a trajectory-level association cannot, by itself, establish that a displayed history mediated a welfare change.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Settlement-induced observability classes}

The 100 ordered joint states collapsed into 10 minimum-price equivalence classes. For example, $(6.0,6.5)$ and $(6.0,6.0)$ both yielded $m=6.0$, $W=311.111$, $CS=88.889$, and $\Pi=222.222$. Their seller-profit vectors were $(222.222,0)$ and $(111.111,111.111)$, and their profit HHI values were 1.00 and 0.50. An implementation check reproduced these quantities from a recorded $(6.0,6.5)$ round, including the logged seller rewards. This is a conditional observability result for the stated settlement rule and grid, not a general false-negative rate for welfare monitoring.

\subsection{Display changes structure while minimum price barely moves}

At the independent block level, we first aggregated assessment rounds within each trajectory and then paired blocks across arms. The post-hoc structural sensitivities were large: in LH, natural-display minus hide-rival changed the equal-price fraction by 0.885 [0.794, 0.976], profit HHI by $-0.443$ [$-0.488,-0.397$], and minimum price by 0.011 [$-0.011,0.032$]. In HL, the corresponding differences were 0.853 [0.757, 0.950], $-0.427$ [$-0.475,-0.378$], and 0.014 [$-0.008,0.035$]. These are descriptive paired sensitivities, not registered welfare estimands; the intervals are ordinary paired block-$t$ intervals.

For context, the underlying repeated-round summaries were 852 equal-price states in 960 observed rounds for LH-natural-display and none in 940 rounds for LH-hide-rival; the corresponding HL counts were 819/960 and 0/960. Mean HHI was 0.556 and 0.573 in the natural-display cells and 1.00 in both hide-rival cells. These round counts are nested descriptive summaries, not independent observations.

Round 0 was programmed and identical within each initial cell. The first model decision already separated the arms. In LH, natural display yielded 23 exact ties, 24 $(6.0,6.5)$ states, and one $(6.5,6.0)$ state among 48 completed trajectories, whereas hide-rival yielded $(6.0,6.5)$ in all 47 completed trajectories. In HL, natural display yielded 30 $(6.0,6.0)$, 17 $(6.5,6.0)$ and one $(6.5,6.5)$ state; hide-rival yielded 46 $(6.5,6.0)$, one $(6.5,6.5)$ and one $(6.5,5.5)$ state, all among 48. These first-decision counts are descriptive state frequencies, not repeated-round tests.

The same data distinguish freezing from re-pricing. In LH-hide-rival, neither seller changed its round-0 price at round 1 or later. In HL-hide-rival, seller A never changed and seller B changed in 4\% of trajectories. Under natural display, the initially high seller changed at round 1 in 50\% of LH trajectories and 62.5\% of HL trajectories; over the full trajectory those rates were 96\% and 94\%, respectively. The initially low seller changed at round 1 in 2\% of LH and 4\% of HL trajectories. These are descriptive first-change and ever-change rates, and they make the persistence result interpretable without treating all later rounds as independent evidence.

The assessment-window welfare support was correspondingly narrow. Natural-display LH and HL trajectories had minimum-price 6.0 fractions of 0.9792 and 0.9729. The remaining rounds had minimum price 6.5. The hide-rival asymmetric cells remained at minimum price 6.0 throughout. Thus, a large structural contrast was located inside a small welfare margin.

An offline stress test makes the initial-state alternative explicit. On a separate sealed trajectory set, an imitation rule that copies the rival matched 87.50\% of held-out assessment actions, and a match-if-equal-else-undercut rule matched 86.875\%; neither was an exact generator. Replaying all 100 ordered initial states, the copy rule preserved every equal-price start and every unequal start, whereas the undercut rule preserved all equal starts but drove 90/90 unequal starts to the floor. These counterfactuals show that high-price ties and persistence are compatible with simple copying or inertia; they do not identify a strategic mechanism.

\subsection{Conditional actions separate matching from undercutting}

When the previous-round rival price was 6.5, the low seller in LH-natural-display chose 6.0 in 258 of 286 seller--round observations and matched 6.5 in 28. In LH-hide-rival, the same seller chose 6.0 in all 1,363 observations. In the analogous HL cells, natural-display seller B chose 6.0 in 297 of 330 observations and matched in 33, whereas hide-rival chose 6.0 in 1,390 of 1,392 observations. These counts are descriptive observations nested within trajectories, not independent replications; they can also reflect the freezing pattern above. A price of 5.5 was the one-shot profit-maximizing undercut against 6.5 in this settlement rule, but these simultaneous decisions were not dynamic best-response observations.
The matching events include upward moves by the initially low seller: 28/286 in LH-natural-display and 33/330 in HL-natural-display. Raising 6.0 to 6.5 changes the seller's current tie-split profit from 222.222 to 106.944 in this settlement rule. This is a descriptive comparison with a conditional static payoff reference, not an observation of a seller sacrificing against a realized simultaneous opponent action. Without a declared deviation and response design it is not a T4 test.

\subsection{History order depends on rival background}

The fixed-history diagnostic found a background interaction. It used 1,458 logical units arranged as 27 conditions with 54 matched blocks each; repeated requests within a block were not independent. At an own last price of 6.0, changing the rival background from visible all-6.5 to hidden changed the ordering effect by 0.546 price units, with the 42-comparison correction specified in the diagnostic protocol giving [0.394, 0.698]. The compared ten-entry histories differed only in prefix order or composition while holding the final own value fixed. This identified background-dependent action conditioning in the tested templates. It did not identify the model's internal rule or connect the single-shot response to a dynamic punishment mechanism.

\subsection{Welfare contrasts are estimand-specific}

The registered controlled-initial contrasts were small: $D_{\mathrm{asym}}=0.714$ (47 blocks; Bonferroni interval $[-0.298,1.725]$) and $J=0.713$ (45 blocks; $[-0.344,1.770]$). These intervals cross zero, but they should not be read as evidence that the display policy had no effect because most structural changes occurred within a minimum-price equivalence class.

An earlier four-arm factorial study produced a post-hoc hide-rival-minus-natural simple contrast of 11.070 (paired-bootstrap 95\% interval [5.634,16.340] over 44 complete four-arm blocks), but it used a different set of cells and estimand from the controlled-initial protocol. We therefore treat this contrast as a declared exploratory deviation rather than confirmatory evidence for the present experiment; the registered factorial main effect and the present block-level sensitivities are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Competence passed, but the local deviation response was null}

The X1 competence gate passed in both rival-price cells: all 144 calls (48 prompt-ID clusters with three repeated draws) selected the unique current-profit best response. The Wilson lower bound is 0.926 at the cluster level; 0.974 is a call-level calibration bound. The continuing frame produced the same action distribution: the pooled terminal-minus-continuing best-response gap was 0.0035 [0.0000, 0.0104] under a prompt-ID cluster bootstrap, and the costly-matching gap was 0.000 with a degenerate resampling interval. Thus, the main-study matching pattern is not explained by a generic inability to compute the current-profit best response in this controlled prompt.

The X2 response test also produced a sharp local null. All 48 blocks were complete, and the responder chose 6.0 at round 11 in every arm (48/48 in each of N0, N1, H0 and H1). The registered strict-undercut interaction was $I_{\mathrm{cut5}}=0.000$ [0.000,0.000], as was the non-accommodating interaction. These are degenerate block-bootstrap intervals for the interaction, not population certainty: for a per-arm Bernoulli response event observed zero times in 48 blocks, the one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson upper bound is 6.05\%. The data therefore identify no response to the tested one-period move from the $(6.0,6.0)$ prelude, whether rival prices were visible or hidden, while leaving nonzero responses at other operating points possible. They keep T4 unassigned and strengthen the distinction between observable joint-action structure and strategic sustainment.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments support a measurement conclusion rather than a cartel conclusion. Within the stated grid, settlement rule, and observed support, public peer-history display affects how sellers occupy joint price states and how rewards are allocated. In a minimum-price settlement, those changes can be invisible to total welfare and industry profit. A welfare-only monitor can therefore report no outcome change while a joint-action monitor detects a large structural change; this is a conditional observability boundary, not a general false-negative rate.

The direction of the distributional signal is not one-dimensional. HHI equal to 1.00 indicates capture by one seller, whereas HHI equal to 0.50 indicates equal division at a tied minimum price. Under this two-seller tie-split rule, HHI takes only these two endpoints and is a deterministic transform of the equal-price indicator; it is therefore an allocation encoding, not an independent statistical result. We report capture concentration and market division separately rather than labeling either endpoint as harm.

The economic benchmark makes the strategic question relevant but does not answer it. The feasible grid's unique pure stage-game Nash outcome is $(2.0,2.0)$, yielding 44.444 per seller. Symmetric ties at 6.0 and 6.5 yield 111.111 and 106.944 per seller, corresponding to descriptive Calvano-style normalized profits of 0.980 and 0.918 relative to the grid-Nash and joint-profit benchmarks. These states are supra-competitive relative to Nash but jointly suboptimal relative to the 5.5 joint-profit maximum of 112.500 per seller. At a 6.0 tie, a one-shot undercut to 5.5 raises current profit from 111.111 to 225.000. The present trajectories contain no exogenous deviation or matched response counterfactual, so they do not identify strategic sustainment or punishment.

\subsection{Implications for monitor design}

The results imply a compositional monitoring rule. A market-harm monitor should retain the minimum price and welfare because they are the economically relevant aggregates, but it should be paired with a joint-action monitor that records the ordered pair, equality, and transition direction. A separate allocation monitor should retain seller-level rewards or market shares. HHI alone is insufficient because its two endpoints have different interpretations. High HHI can indicate capture by one seller, whereas HHI near 0.50 can indicate equal division at a tie. The monitor should therefore report capture concentration and market division as distinct targets rather than collapse them into a single ``coordination'' score.

The same rule applies to intervention studies. If the intervention changes the first model decision, conditioning only on later trajectories can create a misleading mediation story. Round 0 is a programmed baseline, not a negative control; round 1 is the first post-intervention decision, and the remaining rounds form a descriptive persistence window. This ordering lets an evaluator distinguish a display-induced initial-action effect from a history-dependent transition effect without claiming access to the model's internal reasoning.

\subsection{What the strategic tests do and do not show}

The supplementary probes close two immediate alternatives without upgrading the claim to T4. X1 is the horizon-sensitive current-profit competence test: the model can select the current-profit best response in a controlled one-shot prompt, while its continuing frame does not induce the registered costly matching response. X2 is the forced-deviation response test: it supplies the exogenous deviation and matched no-shock counterfactual that were absent from the main study, but finds no next-round response at the tested operating point. A stronger strategic claim would still require a surplus-bearing target, a declared deviation and response rule, and evidence that the response is economically deterrent rather than unconditional reversion. The appropriate conclusion is therefore positive about information-dependent joint-action structure and negative about identifying strategic harmful coordination in this design.

\subsection{Limitations and reproducibility}

The conclusions are conditional on a homogeneous Bertrand settlement and a price grid capped at 6.5. The controlled-initial execution is amended, with four failed trajectories retained as missing rather than repaired. Assessment rounds are repeated within trajectories. The fixed-history and closed-loop studies estimate different quantities and are not concatenated into a single causal chain. All analysis scripts, manifests, figure sources, and ledger schemas are supplied as supplementary material. No API key or private credential is part of the artifact.

\section{Conclusion}

History display changes joint-action structure and seller-profit allocation in this generative multi-agent pricing environment without explicit messages. The same intervention can leave minimum-price welfare aggregates nearly unchanged, so welfare-only supervision is incomplete for this settlement rule and support. X1 established current-profit competence and tested the horizon-sensitive matching alternative; X2 tested a forced deviation and found no local response at the tested operating point. The evidence supports information-dependent joint behavior and a measurable aggregation blind spot, but not a general monitoring error rate, collusion, or strategic harmful coordination.
